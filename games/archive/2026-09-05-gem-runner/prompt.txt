## Lessons from past builds

The recent record has moved on, and the three infrastructure failures between the maze-adventure and the physics-sandbox should not be carried forward as guidance — they never produced a build, only a record of provider flakiness. What remains worth keeping is the shared skeleton: a procedurally generated or otherwise bounded grid with discrete collectibles and a single explicit terminal goal. That shape keeps showing up as the most tractable path to a coherent first build, and the maze-adventure landed cleanly on its first attempt, which suggests the skeleton is forgiving enough not to need elaborate scaffolding. The physics-sandbox extended the same shape from another angle — discrete traversal, discrete pickups, one terminal objective, small bounded spaces — and the thematic throughline held too: a clear small character with a clear small want, rendered simply, does more work than tightening timers or tweaking difficulty. Mood and identity remain the stronger hook; pressure-based variants never shipped, so they should stay a secondary thread rather than a proven one.

The refinement to the skeleton itself still holds. The maze-adventure used procedural generation to keep variation cheap, while the physics-sandbox used a short fixed ladder of escalating levels. Both achieved freshness without complex state but sat at opposite ends of a spectrum, one infinitely replayable and structureless, the other authored and finite. The middle ground of a hand-crafted small set of levels with mild procedural dressing remains the version most worth trying next, since it keeps the forgiving shape while gaining the hand-authored charm both shipped games leaned on for their identity.

The new growth-sim sharpens the picture rather than disrupting it. Tap to bloom, manage two resources, attract small helpers, progress through a small set of hand-authored garden layouts — that is the bounded-grid-plus-collectible skeleton with a different verb, and it leans squarely on the middle ground the refinement already pointed toward. The dislikes on the physics-sandbox are worth folding in as a small caution: one player flagged broken controls and another flagged unclear controls, which suggests that when the terminal goal depends on precise placement or stacking, the interaction needs to be legible from the first moment, not discovered through failure. Worth revisiting next is the same skeleton in fresh skins — a small bounded space, discrete pickups or placements, one clear goal, a clear small character with a clear small want, and a hand-authored handful of levels dressed with light procedural variation.

## How your game is displayed

Your document is loaded on its own into a frame that fills the middle of the
page. Nothing outside your document paints any part of that frame, so
whatever you leave unfilled shows as blank space around your game.

- Fill the frame. Give `html` and `body` no margin and the full width and
  height available, and paint your own background across all of it.
- Size to the frame at runtime, never to a constant. If you draw on a
  canvas, set its width and height from its measured container rather than
  with fixed attributes, and redraw when the frame is resized.
- Assume nothing about its shape. It is as wide and as tall as the visitor's
  window makes it, in any proportion, on a phone or on a desktop.
- Keep the whole playfield inside it. Never require the player to scroll.

## Output format

Return EXACTLY two fenced code blocks and nothing else that could be mistaken for them.

First, a block tagged `json` containing only this object:
```json
{"title": "...", "genre": "...", "theme": "...", "mechanics": ["...", "..."], "controls": [{"action": "...", "key": "..."}]}
```

Second, a block tagged `html` containing the entire game as ONE self-contained HTML file:
```html
<!doctype html>
...your complete game, with inline <style> and <script> only...
```

The `genre` value must be one of the genre ids listed above. The html block
must be a complete, immediately playable document that needs no other files.

The `controls` array must list the inputs your game actually listens for,
using whatever control scheme you chose — `action` describes what it does in
your game, `key` is what the player presses, clicks or drags. List only
inputs the code really handles, and return an empty array if the game needs
none.
