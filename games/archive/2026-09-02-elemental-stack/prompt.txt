## Lessons from past builds

The maze-adventure is now old enough that its specifics should stop steering new builds, and both of its surrounding days were pure infrastructure failures — missing html extraction on one provider, a 429 rate limit on another, and empty moderation responses on the others — so they have nothing to add and are dropped entirely. What carries forward is the shape rather than the ingredients: a procedurally generated grid, discrete collectibles, and a single explicit terminal goal remains the most reliable low-risk skeleton in this project's repertoire. It produces tractable scope, immediate player feedback, and enough run-to-run variation to feel fresh without dragging in complex state, and the first successful attempt was coherent end to end, which suggests the shape is forgiving and does not need elaborate scaffolding to land.

The newer published entry extends this picture rather than disrupting it. A platformer on a small set of floating islands, with a single collectible type and a clear reach-the-end goal, lands on the same low-risk skeleton from a different angle: discrete traversal, discrete pickups, one terminal objective. The fact that it shipped on the first attempt reinforces the earlier read that this shape is forgiving. Combined with the maze-adventure, the pattern is clear — small bounded spaces, one verb family, one thing to gather, one place to end up — and that pattern is now the strongest recurring signal in the recent record.

Worth revisiting is the framing around these skeletons. Thematic identity carried more weight than mechanical pressure in both shipped games: geometric critters seeking glowing homes, a curious robot collecting glowing crystals. A clear small character with a clear small want, rendered simply, seems to do more work than tightening timers or tweaking difficulty. Pressure-based variants are a reasonable secondary thread but not the primary one; mood and identity are the stronger hook.

## How your game is displayed

Your document is loaded on its own into a frame that fills the middle of the
page. Nothing outside your document paints any part of that frame, so
whatever you leave unfilled shows as blank space around your game.

- Fill the frame. Give `html` and `body` no margin and the full width and
  height available, and paint your own background across all of it.
- Size to the frame at runtime, never to a constant. If you draw on a
  canvas, set its width and height from its measured container rather than
  with fixed attributes, and redraw when the frame is resized.
- Assume nothing about its shape. It is as wide and as tall as the visitor's
  window makes it, in any proportion, on a phone or on a desktop.
- Keep the whole playfield inside it. Never require the player to scroll.

## Output format

Return EXACTLY two fenced code blocks and nothing else that could be mistaken for them.

First, a block tagged `json` containing only this object:
```json
{"title": "...", "genre": "...", "theme": "...", "mechanics": ["...", "..."], "controls": [{"action": "...", "key": "..."}]}
```

Second, a block tagged `html` containing the entire game as ONE self-contained HTML file:
```html
<!doctype html>
...your complete game, with inline <style> and <script> only...
```

The `genre` value must be one of the genre ids listed above. The html block
must be a complete, immediately playable document that needs no other files.

The `controls` array must list the inputs your game actually listens for,
using whatever control scheme you chose — `action` describes what it does in
your game, `key` is what the player presses, clicks or drags. List only
inputs the code really handles, and return an empty array if the game needs
none.
